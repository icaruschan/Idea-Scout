What Hyperliquid reveals about product-market fit, community ownership, and building crypto businesses that can survive a downturn.

Projects don't usually disappear because of bear markets, they disappear because bear markets expose weaknesses that were already there.

Every cycle follows a familiar pattern.
A project looks unstoppable during a bull market; the numbers are growing, the community is active, the token is performing well.

Then conditions change.
Volume falls, incentives become harder to sustain, treasury values shrink, and users stop showing up.

The market starts asking questions that nobody cared about during the uptrend:
⇥ Was there a real product?
⇥ Were users actually using it?
⇥ Was revenue tied to genuine demand?
⇥ Could the business survive without incentives?

Many projects struggle when those questions arrive.

That's why @HyperliquidX has become such an interesting case study. Not because it has proven itself immune to downturns. No project has.

But because several of the decisions behind its growth address problems that have repeatedly hurt crypto businesses in previous cycles.

Studying those decisions reveals lessons that extend far beyond a single protocol.

Product-Market Fit Is Still the Most Underrated Advantage in Crypto

One of the easiest ways to spot a fragile project is to look at the order in which things were built.

Many projects launch a token first, then they use incentives to attract activity, and then hope real demand eventually appears.

Sometimes it does, but it often doesn't.

The challenge is that incentives can create activity without creating product-market fit.
People will show up for rewards. That doesn't mean they'll stay once the rewards disappear.

Hyperliquid took a different path.

By the time $HYPE launched, the platform already had significant trading volume and an active user base. The token followed adoption rather than trying to create it.

That's a simple distinction, but it's one of the clearest differences between projects built around utility and projects built around distribution.

The broader lesson is straightforward: A token can accelerate demand, but it rarely creates durable demand on its own.

Community Ownership Changes Behavior

Crypto talks constantly about community, but far fewer projects are willing to give communities meaningful ownership.

When HYPE launched, 31% of the supply went directly to the community. The team didn't receive tokens on day one. Venture capital firms weren't given the usual discounted entry.

That doesn't automatically make the distribution perfect, but it created a different starting point.

Many projects spend years trying to manufacture community loyalty after insiders have already captured most of the value. Hyperliquid approached the sequence differently.

Historically, communities that feel aligned with a product tend to contribute differently than communities primarily motivated by short-term rewards.

The next bear market will provide a clearer test of that idea. But the early signs are difficult to ignore.

Transparency Can Be a Product Feature

One of the biggest lessons from the FTX collapse was that opacity creates hidden risk. Many users had no idea how interconnected customer funds, trading firms, and exchange operations had become until everything broke.

Hyperliquid's HLP vault offers an alternative approach. The mechanism, performance, and activities are all visible.

I'm not saying that transparency eliminates risk, it doesn't. But transparency changes how users evaluate risk.

Most projects treat transparency as a governance discussion, but Hyperliquid turned it into part of the product experience.

That's an approach more builders may need to consider. Trust is easier to maintain when users can inspect the system themselves.

Infrastructure Often Scales Better Than Applications

At some point, many successful products face the same question: Should we keep adding features, or should we build systems that allow others to create value?

Hyperliquid's HIP-3 and HIP-4 initiatives suggest the team is increasingly focused on the second path.

HIP-3 allows builders to launch new futures markets through a staking mechanism.
HIP-4 extends the platform into prediction markets.

What's notable isn't simply the new functionality, it's the shift in responsibility. The Hyperliquid team doesn't have to build every market themselves, other participants can do it. That changes how growth happens.

Products grow when teams ship features. Platforms grow when ecosystems start building on top of them. The distinction matters because platform growth often scales differently than product growth.

Every new market launched by an external builder creates activity the core team didn't have to create themselves.

Revenue Quality Matters More Than Revenue Size

Crypto often focuses on headline numbers - revenue, volume, valuation, token performance, etc.

Those metrics matter, but they don't tell the whole story. The more important question is where activity comes from.

If a project's revenue depends entirely on one type of user behavior, it becomes vulnerable when that behavior changes.

One reason Hyperliquid is attracting attention is that its activity is expanding beyond a single category.

The platform now has exposure to Crypto perpetuals, RWA-related markets, and prediction markets.

These aren't independent businesses, they're still connected to the same ecosystem, but they do create multiple sources of activity. That's important because diversification isn't just about growth, it's also about resilience.

Projects with multiple demand drivers tend to have more options when conditions change.

Founder Experience Shapes Product Decisions

Crypto sometimes swings between two extremes. Either founders are treated as heroes, or they're treated as irrelevant. But reality usually sits somewhere in the middle.

@chameleon_jeff's background doesn't guarantee Hyperliquid's success, but it helps explain many of the product decisions.

Before Hyperliquid, he spent years operating in trading environments and building trading businesses. That experience shows up repeatedly in the platform's design.

From execution quality, liquidity, market structure, and capital efficiency. These aren't random priorities, they're reflections of the problems the founder spent years thinking about before building the product.

The lesson isn't that every project needs a founder with a similar background, the lesson is that founder experience often predicts what a team will care about. And what teams care about eventually becomes visible in the product.

What This Means for Builders

Hyperliquid's story is still being written. The platform hasn't experienced every market condition. It hasn't answered every challenge.

There are still legitimate questions around competition, regulation, ecosystem concentration, and long-term sustainability.

But the project highlights several patterns worth paying attention to:
⇥ Build demand before launching a token.
⇥ Give users a reason to stay beyond incentives.
⇥ Make trust easier to verify.
⇥ Think carefully about whether you're building a product or a platform.
⇥ Create multiple sources of activity where possible.
⇥ Align token utility with ecosystem growth.

None of these ideas are unique, and that's partly the point. Many successful businesses inside and outside crypto are built on the same principles.

Hyperliquid didn't invent them, it just applied them consistently.

Conclusion

Every bear market asks the same question: Was there a real business underneath the narrative?

The answer is rarely determined by price. It's usually determined by fundamentals that were visible long before the market started falling.

Whether Hyperliquid ultimately becomes one of crypto's long-term winners remains to be seen.

What is already visible, however, is a collection of decisions that address many of the weaknesses that have repeatedly caused projects to struggle when conditions become difficult.

The obvious markets already have winners. The interesting opportunities are somewhere else.

One of the most common mistakes founders make is assuming a large market automatically means there is room for another competitor.

Usually, it means the opposite.

The larger and more established a market becomes, the more important it is to understand where the major players are strong, where they're weak, and which opportunities they are structurally unable to pursue.

Prediction markets are a good example.

Today, the overwhelming majority of activity flows through @Polymarket and @Kalshi. Together, they account for most of the volume, liquidity, attention, and mindshare in the category.

That's why one of the most interesting questions in prediction markets isn't: How do you beat Polymarket or Kalshi?

It's: Where can you build something they can't?

Most startups don't fail because the market is too competitive, they fail because they spend years building a smaller version of something users already have.

The opportunities that remain in prediction markets reveal a useful lesson for builders in every category.

First, Understand What the Leaders Already Own

When people talk about prediction markets, they often talk about them as though they are a single market.

They're not.

Different users come to prediction markets for different reasons, and the largest platforms have built strong positions around specific behaviors.

Polymarket dominates markets driven by news, politics, and public narratives. These are events people actively discuss and debate, making liquidity easier to attract and maintain.

Kalshi has built a strong position around recurring events, particularly sports and other outcomes that fit naturally into daily habits.

Institutional risk management is a third category that receives less attention today but could become one of the largest over time. Here, prediction markets become tools for managing uncertainty rather than expressing opinions. The opportunity is significant, but so are the regulatory and trust requirements.

The important takeaway isn't that these categories are closed, it's that competing directly against major players in their strongest areas is usually the hardest path available.

Most founders start by looking for market gaps, while in reality, user gaps are often more important.

The question shouldn't be: Which market should we enter?

The better question is: What users or use cases are still underserved?

Markets don't create advantages. Users do.

The platforms that succeed usually aren't the ones that find a completely new market, they're the ones that understand a specific group of users better than everyone else.

Prediction markets are no different.

Several distinct user groups contribute to the ecosystem, each bringing something different:
➢ Information Traders: These users have genuine informational advantages. They're political analysts, domain experts, sports researchers. People who believe they know something the market doesn't. They are difficult to attract but incredibly valuable.

Without informed participants, prediction markets become entertainment rather than information systems.

➢ Narrative Commentators: These users influence attention. They may not always possess the deepest expertise, but they have audiences that listen to them. They amplify ideas, drive engagement, and create conversations around markets.

➢ Casual Traders: These are often the easiest users to acquire. They enjoy participation for its own sake. They add liquidity, create activity, and also tend to be the least loyal.

➢ Hedgers: These users aren't looking for entertainment, they're trying to manage risk. They usually arrive after markets mature and liquidity deepens.

➢ Bots and AI Agents: This group receives less attention than it deserves. Many prediction market participants are already automated.

They perform arbitrage, provide liquidity, and monitor pricing inefficiencies. Increasingly, they make decisions based on external data sources.

Most prediction market platforms were designed for humans. That assumption may become increasingly important over time.

Why Participant Mix Matters

One insight that stood out while studying prediction markets is that not all users contribute the same thing to a market.

⇥ Information traders help improve market quality.
⇥ Commentators and creators bring attention.
⇥ Casual traders add liquidity.
⇥ Hedgers often arrive when markets become deep and reliable enough to serve a practical purpose.
⇥ Automated traders help identify inefficiencies and keep markets aligned.

The exact mix varies from platform to platform, but healthy markets tend to attract multiple participant types over time.

Many struggling prediction markets focus heavily on attracting activity without paying enough attention to market quality.

Volume alone doesn't create durable markets. The participants behind that volume matter just as much.

Where New Entrants Still Have Opportunities

The opportunities aren't necessarily where the market leaders are weak, they're sometimes where the leaders are too broad to focus deeply.

➢ Geography Remains Underserved: One opportunity that continues to stand out is geography.

Most prediction market activity today revolves around events that matter primarily to U.S. and global audiences.

But large populations elsewhere have their own elections, sports leagues, economic debates, and cultural moments.

Consider:
⇥ Nigerian elections
⇥ African football
⇥ Indonesian crypto communities
⇥ Regional political outcomes
⇥ Local economic trends

Hundreds of millions of people have strong opinions on these topics, of which many already participate in some form of betting or forecasting. Yet few products are designed specifically for them.

The challenge isn't identifying demand, the challenge is execution.

⇥ How do disputes get resolved when data sources are inconsistent?
⇥ How do users onboard when local payment infrastructure differs from global norms?
⇥ How do platforms establish trust in markets where local context matters?

These questions are difficult enough that many global platforms choose not to prioritize them. That's what keeps the opportunity open.

Some teams are already exploring this direction. @BayseMarkets, for example, is building around prediction market use cases relevant to African audiences.

Whether they ultimately succeed remains to be seen, but their focus highlights a broader point: local knowledge can sometimes be a stronger advantage than scale.

➢ Attention Is Becoming a Tradable Asset: People already spend hours debating creators, trends, narratives, and online influence.

Questions like these seem naturally suited to prediction markets:

⇥ Will this creator reach one million followers?
⇥ Which narrative will dominate the timeline next month?
⇥ Will a trend continue or disappear?

Several teams have already started exploring this category.

Polymarket experimented with attention markets through its partnership with @KaitoAI. The idea attracted interest, but it also exposed difficult questions around manipulation, transparency, and resolution.

⇥ How should outcomes be measured?
⇥ Which data sources should be trusted?
⇥ How do markets remain fair when engagement itself can be artificially influenced?

These questions are still unresolved.

Projects like @TrendleFi are exploring different approaches to the problem, particularly around how outcomes are verified and settled.

The market opportunity isn't proving that people care about attention, they already do. The challenge is building systems people trust enough to put money behind.

➢ The Long Tail Is Larger Than It Looks: Geography is one opportunity. Long-tail markets are another.

Most prediction market volume concentrates around a small number of categories:

⇥ Politics
⇥ Major sports
⇥ Crypto

Everything else receives a fraction of the attention. Yet collectively, niche communities represent a substantial opportunity.

⇥ Local football leagues
⇥ Regional entertainment industries
⇥ Startup fundraising outcomes
⇥ Creator milestones
⇥ Music scenes
⇥ Combat sports outside the mainstream

Individual markets may be too small to support a dedicated platform. Together, however, they represent millions of people with strong opinions and few ways to express them financially.

The opportunity may not be building a platform for a single niche. It may be building infrastructure that allows communities to create and sustain their own markets.

➢ AI-Native Markets: This is the category I find most interesting. Not because it's guaranteed to succeed, but because it's still unclear.

Most discussions about prediction markets focus on what people will trade. A less discussed question is how markets will be accessed.

Today, most prediction market platforms are designed primarily for humans; interfaces, workflows, and resolution processes.

But what happens when a significant portion of activity comes from software rather than people? Considering an increasing share of market activity is already automated.

Market-making systems provide liquidity, arbitrage systems identify pricing inefficiencies, and data-driven trading models react to new information faster than individuals can.

This raises an interesting possibility.

What does a prediction market look like when machine-readable data, APIs, automated execution, and programmatic participation become primary design requirements?

Instead of building a new prediction market, what if the opportunity is building infrastructure for the next generation of participants?

This isn't necessarily a prediction market category, it's an infrastructure layer. And like many infrastructure opportunities, it may end up being more important than it looks today.

What Serious Builders Should Be Asking

Most founders focus on technology first, but the more important questions are often strategic.

Which users are you acquiring first?

The answer shapes everything that follows.

User quality influences market quality → Market quality influences growth → Growth influences sustainability.

Do you have a distribution advantage?

Many opportunities look attractive until founders realize they must build both the product and the audience from scratch.

Emerging markets reward local credibility.
Creator markets reward existing audiences.
Developer-focused products reward technical trust.

The strongest opportunities often align with strengths founders already possess.

Why is this gap still open?

Every open opportunity has a reason behind it. Sometimes it's regulation, sometimes it's trust, distribution, or simply poor timing.

Identifying the gap is easy, but understanding why it still exists is much harder.

The Lesson Beyond Prediction Markets

The most valuable takeaway from studying prediction markets has little to do with prediction markets, it's more about competition.

When major players dominate a category, the goal usually isn't to build a better version of what already exists. It's to find users, problems, or workflows that the leaders are unlikely to prioritize. That's where new entrants have room to create value.

Polymarket and Kalshi have built powerful positions. But that doesn't mean the market is closed, it just means that the obvious opportunities are.

The next generation of prediction market companies will probably not emerge by competing head-on with the leaders. They will emerge by serving users, communities, and use cases that the leaders are structurally unable to focus on.

That's a lesson worth remembering far beyond prediction markets.

The truth about being AI native. I'll break it down.
 
Everyone is walking around saying they’re “AI-native” now, which mostly means someone on the team has a ChatGPT tab open and the head of marketing made a custom GPT called “Brand Voice Assistant.”

Cute.

Useful, even.

But not AI-native.

That’s the difference people keep missing. An AI-native company is not a company that uses AI. It is a company that has been rebuilt so AI can actually operate inside it. The business is structured, documented, permissioned, and instrumented in a way that agents can understand. The company has made itself legible to machines.

That sounds boring until you realize it might be the single biggest business advantage of the next decade.

Because most companies are not legible to machines. Most companies are barely legible to their own employees.

The CRM says one thing. The Slack thread says another. The real customer history lives in someone’s inbox. The pricing logic is in a spreadsheet called “Final_v7_NEW.” The refund policy is in a Notion doc nobody trusts. The sales process is “talk to Sarah, she knows how we do enterprise.” The onboarding flow is five tools, three humans, two approval steps, and one founder who still gets pulled into random edge cases because nobody ever turned judgment into a system.

Then these companies ask, “Why can’t AI do more for us?”

Because AI cannot run on vibes.

It can’t operate a business where the truth is scattered across people, tools, habits, exceptions, and institutional memory. Agents need context. They need clean inputs. They need rules. They need access. They need boundaries. They need to know what good looks like. They need to know when to act and when to ask.

Most companies have spent twenty years buying software, but they have not spent twenty years designing an operating system. They have a pile of tools, not a machine.

That is why the number of truly AI-native companies is probably shockingly small. My guess is there are maybe 1,000 companies on earth doing $5M+ ARR that are actually AI-native in the real sense. Not “we use copilots.” Not “we automated some emails.” I mean companies where the core workflows are designed for agents to execute and humans to supervise.

Maybe the number is 500. Maybe it’s 2,000. The exact number matters less than the conclusion.

Almost nobody is doing this yet.

Despite all the noise, despite all the funding announcements, despite every SaaS homepage being rewritten with the word “agentic,” the field is basically empty.
 
The first useful distinction is this: AI-assisted companies use AI at the edges. AI-native companies redesign the center.

An AI-assisted company asks, “Where can we add AI to save time?”

An AI-native company asks, “How should this workflow exist if agents are doing the first 80%?”

That second question changes everything.

Take customer support. In a normal company, a support ticket arrives, a human reads it, searches for context, checks the account, remembers policy, writes a response, maybe asks engineering, maybe escalates, maybe forgets to tag the reason properly. It’s a human-driven process with software sprinkled around it.

In an AI-native company, the ticket enters a system an agent can understand. The agent reads the customer history, checks plan limits, reviews prior tickets, consults policy, drafts a response, recommends an action, and either resolves the issue or sends it to a human with the exact reason it needs judgment. The human is not the search engine, router, and copywriter. The human is the reviewer of ambiguity.

That is a very different company.

The old way is an SDR Googling a prospect, guessing at personalization, writing a mediocre email, updating Salesforce because their manager nags them, then passing half-context to an AE. Legal is the same. Recruiting is the same. Finance is the same. Claims processing is the same. Account management is the same. Research is the same.

The pattern repeats everywhere: agents do the structured work, humans handle taste, trust, judgment, relationships, and exceptions.

That is not a small productivity improvement. That is a new management model.

For the last hundred years, the default way to scale a company was to hire more people, create departments, add managers, buy software, and invent processes to coordinate the mess. Every new layer solved one problem and created three more. The company got bigger, but it also got slower. More meetings. More handoffs. More “who owns this?” More internal gravity.

AI-native companies will scale differently.

They will not look like traditional companies with a chatbot bolted on. They will look like small teams operating large fleets of specialized agents. A 12-person company will do what used to require 80 people. A 40-person company will compete with a 400-person incumbent. That’s not the point.

We use humans to remember process. We use humans to search folders. We use humans to rewrite the same email. We use humans to chase approvals. We use humans to summarize calls, fill in fields, copy data, classify requests, and ask other humans where something lives.

AI-native companies strip that out.

They preserve the human parts that matter and automate the parts that only existed because software was too dumb to understand context. About the Set

They’re the first Marvel cards ever made. The set was released in 1966 by Donruss, a popular card manufacturer that was eventually acquired by Panini, one of the leading global card makers. At the time, packs cost five cents for 5 cards and a piece of bubble gum. A box contained 24 packs. It’s a simple set: 66 total cards - an even split of 6 Super Heroes each with 11 cards. The card backs when pieced together form a puzzle.

As cards are typically treated, and the consensus view for this set, the first card of each character’s run is considered the first appearance, aka Rookie card.

Scarcity and Condition

I love how this set has a blend of significance, organic scarcity, and condition sensitivity. These cards were printed in 1966, when printing wasn’t as advanced and it was common to see centering issues right out of the pack. On top of that, these were five-cent packs, often intended for kids. At the time, nobody treated them as collectibles or investments; people opened them, stuck them in pockets, bike spokes, traded them - not placing them into sleeves and top loaders like many do today. Because of that, finding cards in great condition is difficult, and makes them even more sought after.

To make the set even more interesting, some cards were designed as “write-your-own” comic panels, where you could fill in the dialogue. Accordingly, many cards literally have writing on them. The only Rookie impacted by this is Hulk with card #45.

All this said, when you look at the selection of this set’s graded cards today, high-grade examples are extremely rare. If you’d like to see the full record of grades for this set, check them out with the major grading companies: PSA | CGC | SGC. These 3 elements - older manufacturing, organic scarcity of cards being lost or damaged over time, and the “write your owns” gives it enormous upside to grow in value given the supply and demand elements I see coming true.
